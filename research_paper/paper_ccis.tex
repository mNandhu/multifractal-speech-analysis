\begin{abstract}
Voice pathology classification is inherently difficult due to the nonlinearity and
multi-scale nature of the underlying pathological speech signals, which cannot be
completely described through traditional acoustic metrics. In this paper, we present a
methodology that addresses both binary (healthy/pathological) and three-class
(healthy/neurological/ structural) classification of vocal pathology. The system employs a fusion of
multifractal features extracted via Multifractal Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (MFDFA),
alongside a set of standardised acoustic features from the extended Geneva Minimalistic
Acoustic Parameter Set (eGeMAPS). Experiments carried out on the Saarbr\"ucken Voice
Database include strict speaker-independent cross-validation as a means of eliminating
data leakage, a technique seldom employed in previous works. The results show that the
system obtains a macro F1 score of \textbf{0.882} and balanced accuracy of 0.875 in
binary pathology classification, and a macro F1 score of \textbf{0.637} in three-class
disease-group classification, both under five-fold speaker-independent cross-validation. Feature
ablation analysis demonstrates that MFDFA-based features, without any conventional acoustic
descriptors, produce a macro F1 score of \textbf{0.866}. The ablation
further exposes a substantial confound: removing speaker age from the best configuration
drops macro F1 from 0.878 to 0.749, indicating that age-related covariance in clinical
voice corpora deserves far more explicit treatment than it typically receives.

\keywords{Voice Pathology Detection \and Multifractal Analysis \and MF-DFA \and
Saarbr\"ucken Voice Database \and Nonlinear Speech Analysis \and Speaker-Independent
Evaluation}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

Voice disorders have high prevalence in the population because of the structural pathology
of the vocal folds, neurogenic disorders in motor coordination, and multiple functional and
psychogenic causes. Proper diagnosis of these disorders requires not only instrumental
procedures such as laryngoscopy but also perceptual evaluation of voice quality by
clinicians. Such methods are precise, but subjectivity and the requirement for specialist
medical expertise complicate the procedure.

Considerable attention has consequently been paid to computerised procedures for detecting
and diagnosing voice disorders, with the aim of reducing subjectivity and providing a
low-cost screening instrument. Computational methods for recognising pathological voices
mostly use standard spectral parameters of the signal such as mel-frequency cepstral
coefficients (MFCCs), fundamental frequency, and perturbation measures. Although these
methods successfully describe spectral properties of speech, they are inefficient for
characterising the temporal and nonlinear dynamics typical of pathological phonation.

Speech generation is a nonlinear process arising from the interaction of several
phenomena --- airstream initiation, vocal fold vibration, and articulatory filtering. In
pathological phonation these dynamics may become unstable, aperiodic, or intermittently
chaotic, producing structure that a linear spectral description does not represent.
Fractal and multifractal theory therefore offers a principled route to quantifying such
dynamics.

For the purposes of this work, \textbf{multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis}
(MFDFA) is applied to speech recordings from speakers with a range of diagnosed voice
disorders. Multifractal analysis of time series is a family of methods that describe the
dynamics of the studied object in terms of its scaling properties over a range of time
scales. A monofractal object is described by a single scaling exponent; a multifractal
description characterises a more complex signal through a spectrum composed of many local
scaling exponents, indexed by the moment order $q$ of the analysis.

The central research question of this work is whether the multifractal characteristics
obtained through MFDFA are informative for detecting speech pathology, and whether they
serve as complementary indicators of pathology alongside standard acoustic parameters.

Three aspects distinguish this work from prior fractal-based voice pathology studies.
First, we employ the full multifractal spectrum from MFDFA rather than a scalar fractal
dimension estimate, providing richer scaling information across multiple moment orders.
Second, we address both \textbf{binary} (healthy vs.\ pathological) and
\textbf{three-class} (healthy vs.\ neurological vs.\ structural) classification within a
single unified pipeline, whereas most prior studies focus exclusively on binary detection.
Third, we enforce strict speaker-independent cross-validation using stratified grouped
$k$-fold partitioning, ensuring recordings from the same speaker never span training and
test splits --- a safeguard that prevents the data leakage shown to produce overly
optimistic performance in many prior works~\cite{saenzlechon2006}. A feature ablation
study further quantifies the standalone and combined contribution of MFDFA to each
classification task, and isolates the effect of speaker age as a potential confound.

The remainder of the paper reviews prior work (Section~\ref{sec:related_work}), formalises
the MFDFA procedure (Section~\ref{sec:mfdfa}), describes the dataset, features, and
splitting strategy (Sections~\ref{sec:dataset}--\ref{sec:pipeline}), presents results
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), and discusses findings and limitations
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

Automatic detection of voice pathologies has traditionally relied on acoustic features such
as MFCCs, perturbation measures, and spectral statistics combined with statistical or
machine learning classifiers~\cite{godino2006,alnasheri2017,rai2025}. Standardised clinical
frameworks have further formalised multi-parameter acoustic assessment: the Dysphonia
Severity Index (DSI) combines jitter, phonation time, and intensity into a perceptually
anchored score~\cite{wuyts2000}, while standardised assessment protocols specify
perturbation and noise measures as a clinical evaluation battery~\cite{dejonckere2001}.
Although these spectral and perturbation-based descriptors capture important phonatory
characteristics, they are inherently linear and cannot represent the nonlinear or
multi-scale temporal irregularities often present in pathological voices. Perturbation
measures in particular presuppose a reliably extractable fundamental period, an assumption
that breaks down precisely in the severely dysphonic cases where automated screening would
be most valuable.

Nonlinear analysis methods have therefore been explored for pathological speech. Little et
al.\ demonstrated that recurrence quantification analysis and fractal scaling properties
extracted from sustained phonations can complement conventional acoustic measures for voice
disorder detection, achieving correct classification performance above
91\%~\cite{little2007b}. Nonlinear dynamical measures --- correlation dimension, Lyapunov
exponents, and entropy statistics --- have further been applied to distinguish healthy and
pathological voices with high accuracy on clinical voice
databases~\cite{henriquez2009,little2007}, reinforcing that temporal complexity measures
provide information not captured by standard spectral descriptors. Recurrence plot-based
characterisation has also been applied to specific vocal fold pathologies including
hyperfunctional dysphonia, laryngitis, and vocal fold polyp, achieving classification
accuracy above 90\% on the Saarbr\"ucken Voice Database~\cite{krishnan2026}.

Beyond binary detection, recent work has addressed multi-class voice disorder
classification. Orozco-Arroyave et al.\ found that different feature families are required
to capture the distinct acoustic signatures of neurological, functional, and laryngeal
disorders~\cite{orozco2015}. Fractal dimension in multiresolution frameworks has also
achieved detection rates above 90\% via per-subband power-spectrum
analysis~\cite{ali2016,kumar2014}.

Multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis extends scalar fractal characterisation by
modelling scaling behaviour across multiple moments and time scales~\cite{mfdfa}.
Multifractal methods have proven effective in other biomedical domains, most notably in
demonstrating the multifractal structure of healthy versus diseased heartbeat
dynamics~\cite{ivanov1999}, where a loss of multifractality accompanies congestive heart
failure. Closer to the present work, multifractal analysis has been applied
directly to speech signals for glottal activity detection, demonstrating the method's
suitability for capturing nonlinear phonatory dynamics~\cite{lal2020multifractal}.
Speech-based disease detection has also benefited from principled acoustic feature design:
Tsanas et al.\ developed 132 dysphonia measures from sustained vowels for high-accuracy
Parkinson's disease classification, demonstrating that carefully engineered descriptors can
match or exceed purely data-driven methods in clinical tasks~\cite{tsanas2012}; similarly,
Sai Jahnavi et al.\ showed that excitation source, voice quality, and prosodic features
extracted from sustained vowels can effectively detect Parkinson's disease from speech
alone~\cite{jahnavi2020}. Despite these advances, MFDFA applied to voice pathology
detection --- particularly for multi-class classification --- remains relatively
underexplored.

A critical methodological concern is \textbf{speaker-level data leakage}. S\'aenz-Lech\'on
et al.\ identified that systems evaluated without controlling for speaker identity yield
optimistic estimates that fail to generalise to unseen speakers, and recommended
speaker-independent cross-validation as standard practice~\cite{saenzlechon2006}. Because
a single speaker in most clinical corpora contributes several recordings, a naive random
split allows a classifier to learn speaker identity rather than pathology. Reported
accuracies are therefore not directly comparable unless the partitioning protocol is stated
explicitly. A second concern is demographic confounding: where age covaries with pathology
status in a corpus, a model given access to age --- directly or through age-correlated
acoustic features --- may exploit that covariance rather than the pathology itself.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} quantifies this effect for the present dataset.

\section{Multifractal Formulation}
\label{sec:mfdfa}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

This section formalises the MFDFA procedure~\cite{mfdfa} as applied to the speech signal
and defines the descriptors derived from it.

\subsection{The MFDFA Procedure}

Let $x_k$, $k = 1,\dots,N$, denote the discrete-time speech signal of length $N$ with mean
$\langle x \rangle$. The analysis proceeds in five steps.

\paragraph{Step 1: Profile construction.}
The signal is converted to a cumulative profile by integrating the mean-removed series:
\begin{equation}
Y(i) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{i} \bigl[ x_k - \langle x \rangle \bigr],
\qquad i = 1,\dots,N .
\label{eq:profile}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 2: Segmentation.}
The profile $Y(i)$ is divided into $N_s = \lfloor N/s \rfloor$ non-overlapping segments of
equal length $s$. Because $N$ is generally not an integer multiple of $s$, a short tail of
the profile would otherwise be discarded; the procedure is therefore repeated starting from
the opposite end of the series, yielding $2N_s$ segments in total.

\paragraph{Step 3: Local detrending.}
Within each segment $v$ a least-squares polynomial fit $y_v(i)$ of order $m$ is subtracted
and the residual variance computed, with the segment offset $o(v) = (v-1)s$ for the forward
pass and $o(v) = N - (v - N_s)s$ for the reverse pass:
\begin{equation}
F^2(s,v) \;=\; \frac{1}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{s}
\bigl\{ Y\bigl[o(v) + i\bigr] - y_v(i) \bigr\}^2 .
\label{eq:var1}
\end{equation}
Detrending of order $m$ removes polynomial trends of order $m-1$; this work uses $m = 1$.

\paragraph{Step 4: $q$-order fluctuation function.}
The segment variances are averaged with a moment order $q$, which controls the relative
weight given to segments with large or small fluctuations:
\begin{equation}
F_q(s) \;=\; \left\{ \frac{1}{2N_s}\sum_{v=1}^{2N_s}
\bigl[ F^2(s,v) \bigr]^{q/2} \right\}^{1/q},
\qquad q \neq 0 .
\label{eq:fq}
\end{equation}
The case $q = 0$ is singular and is replaced by its logarithmic limit,
\begin{equation}
F_0(s) \;=\; \exp\!\left\{ \frac{1}{4N_s}\sum_{v=1}^{2N_s}
\ln\bigl[ F^2(s,v) \bigr] \right\}.
\label{eq:f0}
\end{equation}
Positive $q$ emphasises segments containing large fluctuations; negative $q$ emphasises
segments containing small ones. A signal whose large and small fluctuations scale
differently will therefore exhibit $q$-dependent behaviour, which is precisely the
multifractal signature.

\paragraph{Step 5: Scaling exponents.}
For a signal with power-law scaling, $F_q(s)$ behaves as
\begin{equation}
F_q(s) \;\sim\; s^{\,h(q)} ,
\label{eq:scaling}
\end{equation}
so that the generalised Hurst exponent $h(q)$ is obtained as the slope of a straight-line
fit to $\log F_q(s)$ against $\log s$, computed separately for each $q$. A signal is
monofractal when $h(q)$ is constant in $q$; the degree of variation of $h(q)$ quantifies
the strength of multifractality.

\subsection{Singularity Spectrum}

The generalised Hurst exponent relates to the classical R\'enyi (mass) exponent $\tau(q)$
through
\begin{equation}
\tau(q) \;=\; q\,h(q) - 1 .
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
A Legendre transform of $\tau(q)$ yields the singularity strength $\alpha$ and the
singularity spectrum $f(\alpha)$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha \;=\; \frac{d\tau(q)}{dq},
\qquad
f(\alpha) \;=\; q\,\alpha - \tau(q) .
\label{eq:legendre}
\end{equation}
The resulting $f(\alpha)$ curve is typically a downward-opening arc. Its horizontal extent
\begin{equation}
\Delta\alpha \;=\; \alpha_{\max} - \alpha_{\min}
\label{eq:width}
\end{equation}
measures the richness of the multifractal structure: a wide spectrum indicates a broad
range of local scaling behaviours, whereas $\Delta\alpha \to 0$ indicates a monofractal
signal. The asymmetry of the arc about its peak $\alpha_0$ (the value of $\alpha$ at which
$f(\alpha)$ is maximal) is captured by
\begin{equation}
A \;=\; \frac{\alpha_0 - \alpha_{\min}}{\alpha_{\max} - \alpha_0} ,
\label{eq:asym}
\end{equation}
with $A > 1$ indicating a left-skewed spectrum dominated by large fluctuations and $A < 1$
indicating a right-skewed spectrum dominated by small ones.

\subsection{Derived Descriptors and Parameter Selection}

Twelve scalar descriptors are computed per recording from the curves $h(q)$, $\tau(q)$,
$\alpha(q)$ and $f(\alpha)$: the mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum of $h(q)$;
the mean and standard deviation of $\tau(q)$; the mean and standard deviation of $\alpha$;
and the four spectrum scalars $\Delta\alpha$, $\alpha_0$, $f(\alpha_0)$ and $A$ defined
above. These form the multifractal feature block used in all subsequent experiments.

The moment orders span $q \in [-5, 5]$ in steps of $0.5$, giving 21 values --- wide enough
to resolve $q$-dependence without entering the numerically unstable regime at large $|q|$,
where the moment average in Eq.~(\ref{eq:fq}) becomes dominated by a handful of extreme
segments. Forty analysis scales are used, spaced logarithmically between 16 samples and
$N/4$; the lower bound keeps enough points for a meaningful polynomial fit, and the upper
bound guarantees at least four segments per scale. Detrending order is fixed at $m = 1$.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

The experiments in this study use recordings from the \textbf{Saarbr\"ucken Voice Database
(SVD)}~\cite{svd}, a widely used clinical corpus containing healthy and pathological
voices. The original audio is stored in the proprietary \texttt{.nsp} format and is
converted to standard \texttt{.wav} for preprocessing and feature extraction. Although SVD
contains multiple vowels and connected-speech recordings, this work focuses on the
sustained vowel \textit{/a/} at normal pitch (\texttt{a\_n}) because it provides a stable
phonation condition for cross-speaker comparison and avoids the confound of differing
linguistic content across speakers.

Table~\ref{tab:dataset_stats} summarises the dataset used in the experiments.

The \texttt{a\_n} subset is class-imbalanced, which is typical for clinical speech datasets.
Pathology classes with insufficient speaker representation for reliable

neurological, structural, and functional aetiologies: \textbf{Parkinson's Disease}

\textbf{Hyperfunctional Dysphonia} (strained voice from excessive laryngeal tension);
\textbf{Psychogenic Dysphonia} (voice disorder of psychological aetiology without organic
pathology); and \textbf{Functional Dysphonia} (dysphonia from maladaptive laryngeal
behaviour without identifiable organic cause).

\section{Feature Extraction}
\label{sec:features}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

Four complementary feature families totalling \textbf{107 features} are extracted from each
audio sample, spanning multifractal characteristics, standardised paralinguistic features,
nonlinear complexity measures, and speaker metadata.

\subsection{Multifractal Features}

To capture the nonlinear and multiscale dynamics present in pathological speech signals,
multifractal descriptors are extracted using MFDFA as formalised in
Section~\ref{sec:mfdfa}. From the multifractal spectrum, \textbf{12 statistical
descriptors} are derived from the generalised Hurst exponent, the R\'enyi scaling exponent,
and the singularity spectrum (Table~\ref{tab:mf_descriptors}). The final MFDFA
configuration uses first-order detrending, a $q$ range of $[-5,5]$ with step $0.5$, and 40
analysis scales.

\subsection{OpenSMILE Features}

Standardised acoustic features are extracted using the OpenSMILE toolkit with the
\textbf{extended Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set (eGeMAPSv02)}, contributing
\textbf{88 parameters} capturing prosodic, spectral-shape, voice-quality, and temporal
characteristics~\cite{opensmile}. eGeMAPS is used in preference to larger brute-force
feature sets because its parameters were selected for theoretical interpretability and
cross-corpus stability, which matters when the number of unique speakers is on the order of
one thousand.

\subsection{NeuroKit2 Features}

To further characterise nonlinear signal dynamics, \textbf{seven additional features} are
extracted using the NeuroKit2 library: Shannon entropy, approximate entropy, sample
entropy, Petrosian fractal dimension, Sevcik fractal dimension, skewness, and kurtosis.
These represent the class of scalar complexity measures that MFDFA generalises, so their
marginal contribution in the ablation study indicates whether the full multifractal
spectrum adds information beyond single-valued complexity estimates.

\subsection{Speaker Metadata}

Speaker metadata from the SVD --- \textbf{age} (derived from recording and birth dates) and
\textbf{biological sex} --- are incorporated as \textbf{2 auxiliary features} and retained
by default across all configurations. Because age covaries with pathology status in this
corpus, its individual contribution is isolated in the ablation study
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) and discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Data Pipeline and Splitting Strategy}
\label{sec:pipeline}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

The overall preprocessing workflow is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:data_pipeline}. The
raw \texttt{.nsp} recordings are converted to \texttt{.wav}, indexed through a metadata
manifest, normalised, and then processed by the four feature-extraction modules before the
resulting feature tables are merged into a unified machine learning dataset. To minimise
variability and within-speaker duplication, all reported experiments use only the sustained
vowel \texttt{a\_n}.

A critical concern in clinical voice datasets is \textbf{speaker-level leakage}. We
therefore use stratified grouped $k$-fold cross-validation with speaker identity as the
grouping variable, so that all recordings from a given speaker remain in the same fold
while class proportions are approximately preserved across folds. Rare classes are removed
when necessary for stable training, and class imbalance is handled through filtering or
downsampling of dominant groups. Final performance is reported as the average over five
speaker-grouped folds.

Four classifiers are evaluated: $\ell_2$-regularised logistic regression as a linear
baseline, random forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM. Class imbalance is additionally addressed
at the classifier level through balanced class weighting. Preprocessing differs by model
family. Tree-based models receive the numeric features unaltered, with missing values
preserved and handled natively by the learner. The linear model receives numeric features
that are mean-imputed with a missingness indicator, transformed by a Yeo--Johnson power
transform with standardisation to reduce skew, and filtered to remove zero-variance
columns. Categorical features are imputed by mode and one-hot encoded in both cases. All
preprocessing steps are contained within a pipeline that is fitted on the training fold
only and then applied to the held-out fold, so that no test-fold information influences
preprocessing.

\section{Experimental Setup and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

This section evaluates the proposed framework, assessing multifractal feature
discriminability and its contribution when combined with conventional acoustic descriptors.
All experiments are speaker-independent, ensuring no speaker appears in both training and
test splits. The binary, three-class, and ablation experiments
(Tables~\ref{tab:binary_results}--\ref{tab:ablation_binary}) use five folds; the
per-disease experiments of Section~\ref{subsec:per_disease} operate on much smaller
per-condition cohorts and the number of splits is reduced to two, as noted there.
Performance is reported using accuracy, balanced accuracy, and macro-averaged F1 score;
balanced accuracy and macro F1 are emphasised due to class imbalance. Naive random splits
produced unrealistically high scores in preliminary experiments, confirming the necessity
of speaker-independent evaluation.

%--------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Speaker-Independent Evaluation}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

Only the sustained vowel token \texttt{a\_n} is used to ensure consistency across speakers.
Two classification tasks are considered: (1)~binary classification of healthy vs.\
pathological speakers, and (2)~three-class classification of healthy vs.\ neurological vs.\
structural cases.

The binary classification task serves as a screening scenario where the goal is to detect
the presence of a voice disorder. Table~\ref{tab:binary_results} summarises the results
obtained using five-fold speaker-grouped cross-validation.

\subsection{Disease Group Classification}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

While detecting the presence of pathology is one objective, a more demanding task involves
classifying types of voice disorder according to their clinical cause. In this work,
pathologies are grouped from a clinical perspective into neurological and structural
categories, and the classifier must additionally separate both from healthy phonation,
giving a three-class problem.

Neurological disorders arise from abnormalities in the neural regulation of the vocal
folds, whereas structural disorders arise from abnormalities of the vocal fold tissue
itself. Both ultimately manifest as disturbed phonation, and separating them from the
acoustic signal alone is correspondingly difficult.

The neurological group comprises Parkinson's disease, recurrent laryngeal nerve paralysis,
and spasmodic dysphonia; the structural group comprises vocal fold nodules, vocal fold
polyp, and laryngitis. After speaker-level filtering the task comprises 478 healthy, 277
neurological, and 201 structural samples.

The results reveal several findings. First, the multifractal features alone produce a macro
F1 of 0.866, meaning that the multifractal properties of the speech signal carry
substantial information about the state of the phonatory system. Adding OpenSMILE features
to the multifractal ones yields only a small improvement (0.866 to 0.878), indicating
partial but incomplete redundancy between the two families.

Second, adding NeuroKit2 features leads to essentially no improvement, indicating that the
information carried by scalar complexity measures is largely subsumed by the multifractal
spectrum. This is the expected relationship: MFDFA generalises single-exponent fractal
characterisation, so a monofractal descriptor should add little once the full spectrum is
available.

Third, and most consequentially, removing age from the MFDFA+OpenSMILE configuration
reduces macro F1 from 0.878 to 0.749 --- a drop of nearly 13 points. Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
discusses the interpretation of this result at length. The comparative results across all
configurations are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ablation_results}.

%--------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Multifractal Features as a Standalone Representation}

The clearest result of the ablation is that the twelve multifractal descriptors, together
with the two speaker-metadata features retained across all configurations, reach a macro F1
of 0.866 --- within 1.2 points of the best configuration, which adds 88 eGeMAPS parameters
on top. Two caveats apply: speaker metadata is present here, so 0.866 is not attributable
to the multifractal descriptors in isolation, and identifying which descriptors drive the
performance would require a feature-importance analysis left to future work. The drop to
0.637 on the three-class task reflects both a harder decision and the grouping itself ---
neurological and structural disorders converge on a small set of perceptual outcomes
through different physiological routes, so the acoustic evidence for the route is weaker
than the evidence for the outcome, consistent with the finding that distinct feature
families are required for distinct disorder classes~\cite{orozco2015}.

\subsection{The Age Confound}

The 13-point drop in macro F1 when age is removed is the most important caveat in this
work, and we treat it as a finding rather than an inconvenience. Under a benign reading,
age is a legitimate clinical covariate: voice disorders become more prevalent with age, and
a screening system deployed in practice would have access to patient age, so including it
is neither cheating nor unrealistic. Under a sceptical reading, the SVD pathological cohort
is simply older than its healthy control cohort as an artefact of recruitment, and a large
part of the reported 0.878 reflects the model learning that demographic gap rather than the
pathology.

The evidence does not cleanly separate these. What can be said is that the 0.749 figure
obtained without age is the more defensible estimate of what the acoustic and multifractal
features contribute on their own, and that the gap between 0.749 and 0.878 should be read
as a measure of demographic confounding in the corpus rather than as model performance. We
report both figures for this reason. A stronger design would match healthy and pathological
cohorts on age at the sampling stage; this is not possible within SVD without discarding a
large fraction of the data, but it is the appropriate next step on a larger corpus.

This concern generalises beyond the present study. Where prior work reports high accuracy
on SVD without stating whether demographic metadata was included, the reported figure is
difficult to interpret, in much the same way that accuracy reported without a stated
speaker-partitioning protocol is difficult to interpret~\cite{saenzlechon2006}.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations bound these conclusions. All results come from a single corpus, with
recording conditions and a German-language speaker population held constant throughout, so
generalisation to other corpora and languages is untested; cross-corpus validation is the
most important outstanding experiment. Restricting analysis to the sustained vowel
\texttt{a\_n} buys cross-speaker comparability at the cost of discarding connected speech,
where intermittent disorders such as spasmodic dysphonia are most apparent. Age covaries
with pathology status in this corpus, and the study does not disentangle the two. SVD
provides diagnostic categories but not graded severity, so the analysis cannot distinguish
a model that detects severe cases reliably and mild cases poorly from one that performs
uniformly. Finally, downsampling of dominant classes reduces the effective training set
size: five of the nine conditions in Table~\ref{tab:per_disease} contribute fewer than one
hundred samples per class, and vocal fold nodules only 17, so those rows should not be
treated as reliable point estimates.

%--------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

This paper investigated multifractal descriptors obtained via MFDFA for automated voice
pathology detection on the Saarbr\"ucken Voice Database. MFDFA features were combined with
conventional acoustic (eGeMAPS) and complexity-based descriptors, and evaluated under
strict speaker-independent cross-validation to ensure generalisability to unseen speakers.

The approach achieves a macro F1 of \textbf{0.882} for binary pathology detection and
\textbf{0.637} for three-class healthy/neurological/structural classification. The ablation
study confirms that the MFDFA descriptors reach a macro F1 of \textbf{0.866} without any
conventional spectral features, and that scalar complexity measures add nothing once the
full multifractal spectrum is available. The same ablation exposes a 13-point dependence on
speaker age that we report explicitly rather than absorb into the headline figure.

Overall, this study demonstrates that multifractal descriptors are a compact and effective
complement to traditional spectral features for automated voice-pathology screening,
particularly in binary detection tasks, while fine-grained aetiological classification
remains an open problem. Future work should validate the approach on larger independent
cohorts with age-matched controls, extend the analysis to connected speech and multi-token
protocols, and investigate which regions of the multifractal spectrum carry the most
clinically informative signal.
